# Canonical Concept Registry

Short book-level meanings. Detailed refinements belong to the chapters that introduce them.

## Civilizational coherence
Capacity of a large heterogeneous social system to make distributed capacities discoverable and functionally composable so they can become coordinated action and, through feedback and memory, greater future capacity.

## Distributed intelligence
Knowledge, perception and judgment distributed across people, institutions and contexts. Broader than credentialed expertise; narrower than all human capacity.

## Distributed potential
The wider set of knowledge, skills, judgment, resources, experience, initiative and institutional capability that could contribute to collective action.

Canonical relation:

\[
DistributedIntelligence \subset DistributedPotential
\]

The title foregrounds distributed intelligence; the causal theory usually operates on the broader distributed-potential category.

## Collective agency
Effective capacity of multiple actors to transform distributed perception, knowledge, judgment, skills and resources into coordinated effects in the world.

## Cumulative collective agency
Collective agency that not only produces present outcomes but preserves or increases capacity available for future action.

## Loss
Situation in which existing capacity fails to become available for useful collective action. Loss does not require destruction of the capacity itself.

## Composition
Combination of distinct capabilities such that they produce a joint effect without necessarily being absorbed into one institution.

## Discoverability
Ability of relevant knowledge, people, artifacts or capacities to be found when needed.

## Functional composability
Ability of discovered capacities to work together under relevant technical, semantic, institutional and authority constraints.

## Interface
Sociotechnical relation that converts between actors, capabilities, institutions, meanings or stages of collective action.

## Functional centrality
The degree to which discovery, navigation, routing, composition, legibility or shared services converge through a common center. Functional centrality can be high without giving that center final sovereignty over truth, institutions or politics.

## Articulation hub
A strong common interface that makes distributed capacities discoverable and composable across knowledge, people, groups, deliberation, decision, execution and memory. Its purpose is to compress fragmentation, not to own every underlying capability.

## Terminal dependency
A condition in which practical continuation depends on one center, operator or service to such a degree that failure, capture, refusal or disappearance of that center makes independent operation non-credible.

## Recursive centrality
An architecture in which a strong hub can be replicated as a whole, so each replica becomes a center at its own scale and may later federate with other centers.

## Coordination spine
The project term for a shared orchestration layer that links specialized modules into a coherent loop. Current Kintsugi documentation describes Konnaxion in this role.

## Mimic
Reimplement a useful external pattern natively when coherent UX, sovereignty, auditability or long-term control matter.

## Annex
Integrate a mature external capability as a replaceable sidecar/adapter while preserving ownership boundaries and avoiding dual truth.

## Selective coupling
Creation of useful interfaces without maximizing all possible connections.

## Bottleneck
Transition whose low capacity limits a larger chain.

## Correction latency
Time between the point at which an error could reasonably be detected and effective correction.

## Path dependence
Dependence of present options on previous institutional, technical or social choices.

## Lock-in
Persistence produced partly by switching costs, dependency, convention or accumulated advantage.

## Infrastructural regime
Rules, incentives, standards and accumulated capacities that make certain modes of behavior—such as competition or collaboration—easier to reproduce.

## Contextual competence
Competence understood relative to person, domain, task, context and time rather than as one global property.

## Credibility
Contextual assessment of how much trust a contribution or source merits, informed by competence, evidence, reliability, provenance and relevant conflicts.

## Epistemic mobility
Capacity for recognized epistemic standing to increase, decrease or change as new evidence of performance appears.

## Evidence portfolio
Collection of attestations and demonstrated outputs relevant to estimating competence.

## Attestation
Attributable claim by an actor or institution about a qualification, event, performance or relationship.

## Portable credibility
Ability to carry underlying evidence and attestations across systems so credibility can be reconstructed without one central scoring authority.

## Domination
Condition in which an actor or structure can materially alter others' options, access, standing or operating conditions while those affected lack effective means of verification, contestation, substitution or exit.

## Capture
Conversion of control over a legitimate function into durable influence beyond its intended mandate.

## Non-terminal capture
Design objective in which capture of one institution or instance does not become irreversible control over the whole ecosystem.

## Verification
Ability to inspect what a system did and why.

## Contestation
Ability to challenge a decision, rule, classification or sanction in a way that can have real consequence.

## Substitution
Ability to use another mechanism, operator, lens, registry or instance.

## Credible exit
Ability to leave while retaining enough data, knowledge, evidence, history and capacity to continue elsewhere.

## Technical forkability
Practical ability to create an independent technical branch.

## Social forkability
Ability of a technical alternative to acquire enough legitimacy, participation and recognition to become a real substitute.

## Credible recovery
Restoration of a functional and socially usable alternative after capture or failure.

## Open good
An open functional good whose useful portability extends beyond source code to enough knowledge, data, configuration, documentation and operational capability to permit independent use or substitution where rights allow.

## Cumulative knowledge
Knowledge whose structure, provenance, context and history allow later actors to begin from a reusable inheritance rather than reconstructing prior work.

## Knowledge artifact
Bounded object carrying enough content, structure, provenance and context to remain interpretable and reusable outside its original environment.

## Knowledge sovereignty
Capacity to retain, inspect, move, interpret and reuse legitimately held knowledge independently of the continued existence or permission of the original hosting system.

## Knowledge lineage
Traceable history of derivation, modification, fork and merge among knowledge artifacts.

## Structured pluralism
Coexistence of divergent claims, models and authorities while preserving enough evidence, provenance, scope, uncertainty and history to compare them without treating them as epistemically equal.

## Epistemic status
Declared status of a claim such as observation, hypothesis, interpretation, reference, contested, refuted, fictional or unresolved.

## Reference authority
Actor or institution whose recognition is useful within a stated domain and scope without becoming universal truth authority.

## Semantic coherence
Capacity of actors, languages and institutions to preserve enough conceptual identity, relation, context, uncertainty and provenance for knowledge to remain mutually intelligible and composable.

## Semantic interoperability
Ability to relate meanings across languages, ontologies or institutions without requiring identical vocabularies or false equivalence.

## Semantic mapping
Explicit relationship between two concepts or structures of meaning: equivalent, approximate, broader, narrower, overlapping, contested or without known equivalent.

## Semantic fidelity
Degree to which a representation or language realization preserves relevant relations of meaning.

## Local ontology
Conceptual structure maintained by a community or institution that can interoperate through mappings without being absorbed into a universal ontology.

## Collective judgment
Structured reading of distributed contributions designed to support understanding, comparison or decision.

## Canonical participation facts
Underlying participation events or signals preserved separately from derived interpretations.

## Lens
Explicit reproducible rules selecting, grouping, filtering or weighting the same underlying data to answer an interpretive question.

## Parallel readings
Multiple declared interpretations of a common participation record.

## Baseline reading
Simple reference reading, often one-person-one-vote in democratic contexts, against which other readings can be compared.

## Attention routing
Allocation of scarce human or institutional attention to signals, problems or people according to context and function.

## Agenda power
Capacity to influence which questions receive enough attention to enter meaningful deliberation or action.

## Attention mobility
Ability of new evidence, issues or contributors to acquire attention rather than being permanently excluded by incumbent rankings.

## Expert advice
Structured recommendation grounded in relevant evidence and competence but not itself the sovereign decision.

## Decision authority
Actor or institution possessing legitimate authority to make the relevant decision.

## Decision rationale
Recorded reasons for a decision, particularly when it diverges from major advice.

## Advisory channel
Institutional path by which evidence and expert judgment reach an authority without automatically becoming command.

## Execution
Transformation of a decision into assigned, resourced and coordinated work in the world.

## Operational coherence
Capacity to translate authorized decisions into coordinated execution with feedback and adaptation.

## Replication
Creation of an independently operable instance from a common kernel, specification or artifact.

## Federation
Optional relationship among sovereign instances for selected forms of exchange, coordination or synchronization.

## Sovereignty
Practical capacity of an instance or institution to operate, govern and preserve relevant functions without hidden dependence on an external controller.

## Fork
Independent continuation of a lineage from an identifiable common state.

## Bridge
Selective interface restoring necessary interoperability between divergent branches or systems.

## Multi-scale institution
Institutional architecture in which information, authority and execution may reside at different scales while remaining coordinated.

## Functional subsidiarity
Placement of a function at the lowest or most appropriate scale capable of performing it adequately under relevant externalities and dependencies.

## Selective permeability
Ability of a boundary to allow specified information, knowledge, actions or relations to pass while keeping others local.

## Determinism
Property by which the same canonical inputs, rules and versions produce the same canonical outputs.

## Optional AI
Design stance in which AI can assist analysis, construction or interpretation without becoming necessary for canonical core operation.

## Distributed mastery
Human capability to understand, maintain, repair, teach and evolve the system across more than a small founding group.

## Founder non-dependence and independent reproducibility
Practical property whereby people outside the founding group can understand, reproduce, operate, maintain, teach, fork, adapt or replace the relevant system/model without unique founder knowledge or continuing founder permission. It does not require a creator-owned channel to remove its founder or to persist after the creator stops operating it. For UCKK specifically, the original UCKK may remain its creator's channel while derivative institutions are explicitly encouraged to replicate and retheme the model independently.

## Legible coherence
Capacity of participants to perceive enough of the relations among components, purposes, constraints and pathways to orient themselves within the whole.

## Narrative mediation
Use of stories, characters, metaphors or fictional scenarios to make complex architecture perceptible, memorable, criticizable or actionable.

## Narrative memory
Cultural memory that preserves salient patterns and warnings; complementary to, but not a substitute for, documentary memory.

## Cultural contestability
Narratively and culturally supported ability to recognize a deviation from shared invariants and mobilize ordinary mechanisms of contestation.

## The Surreal
A particular project method: move from real problems into fictional/surreal representation and back toward an expanded sense of possible action. It is an implementation of narrative mediation, not a universal requirement of civilizational coherence.
